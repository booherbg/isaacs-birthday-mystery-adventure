How we built Isaac's Birthday Mystery Adventure

A post-mortem: one dad, one AI pair-programmer (Claude, in Claude Code), one afternoon. It's a pixel-art mystery that reveals an 8-year-old's birthday present, and then becomes the present's own mini-game.

What it is

Isaac turns 8. The family is moving to a new house this week, and a trampoline is coming with it. The game is a 5-ish minute side-scroller built from real places and real family. Isaac wakes up in his room, grabs Big Bunny, and meets Freida the calico in a Pokémon-card-style "wild FREIDA appeared!" battle. He scooters through the neighborhood to Brunsdale Fields, where Dad pitches (Wii-Sports vibes). Then he picks a path: out-trick the Creeper Crew at Dike East Skate Park while Mom judges, or bike the Red River to Lions Conservancy Park, where Mom (Audubon, binoculars up) watches him reel in a mooneye and a channel catfish with a catfish bell on the rod. Then Island Park Pool on 7th St, where Sunny cannonballs into the pool, and finally the new house. Three clues (BIG, BOUNCY, in the BACKYARD) lead to a giant present: IT'S A TRAMPOLINE. His first bounce becomes Trampoline Time, the replayable part: backflips, 360s, the worm, combos up to x8, sky to outer space, and unlockable hats (including his real Pikachu hat and his real fishing hat).

It runs on phones (a touch pad), laptops (keyboard) and a USB gamepad (Gamepad API, including his generic Logitech Precision).

How it's built

No build, no dependencies

Plain HTML + ES modules served straight from GitHub Pages. index.html, style.css, and 15 small modules: an engine, sprites, art, audio, input, UI, world (the platformer), levels (the story), battle, batting, trampoline, cards, title, save and main. There is nothing to install and nothing to compile. Push, and it's live in about 40 seconds.

Canvas for the world, DOM for words

The game renders into a 256×144 canvas scaled up with image-rendering: pixelated. All text (dialog, menus, clue cards, the Pokémon-style foe cards with a CSS holo shimmer) is regular HTML layered on top. That keeps the words crisp and readable for an 8-year-old at any screen size, which a pixel font can't do on a phone. In portrait, the dialog moves into the empty space below the game.

Pixel art as code

Every sprite is an array of strings plus a palette. build() adds a 1px dark outline automatically, so only the fills need drawing. Isaac is assembled from head, body and leg parts, which gives walk, ride, bike, cheer and tuck poses without redrawing his curls each time. Sunny was generated procedurally (overlapping blobs, then a curl dither) after the hand-drawn version looked like "a generic brown dog." Both houses are drawn from Blaine's photos with rectangles and scanline triangles. The house numbers were left out on purpose, because the site is public. A dev page renders every sprite at 4× so the AI could look at its own art and iterate.

All sound is synthesized

Web Audio only: pulse waves (12.5/25/50% duty, built from Fourier series with createPeriodicWave), triangle bass and noise drums, driven by a tiny step sequencer. Songs are written as strings like "C5:2 C6 A5 G5 E5 G5:3". There's a stadium-organ Take Me Out to the Ball Game and Happy Birthday (both public domain), Toniebox tunes, and originals for each place. There are about 45 sound effects, from Sunny's bark and Freida's meow to the catfish bell, the trampoline boing and the party-horn creeper pop. The Red River's babbling-brook bed borrows a trick from Blaine's RollerCoaster Tycoon clone: random tiny sine blips, pitch-skewed low, with upward sweeps.

Input: keyboard, touch, and a $10 gamepad

Keyboard, an on-screen touch pad and the Gamepad API all merge into one per-frame state. The touch pad supports multi-touch (hold ▶ and tap JUMP) and sliding your thumb between arrows, and it relabels itself per moment: JUMP, SWING!, REEL!, OPEN!, FLIP/SPIN/▼. Generic USB pads report "non-standard" mappings, so we shipped a guided controller test page early. Blaine pressed every button and pasted the report, which confirmed the Logitech Precision's layout (d-pad on axes 0/1, face buttons 0–3, shoulders 4–7, start 9). The on-screen hints then use its printed button numbers. Hat-switch d-pads (axis 9) are decoded too.

Story engine

Scenes have enter/update/render. Cutscenes are plain async functions (await say('mom', …), await w.walkTo(dad, 1480), await wait(0.5)) that run on game-time timers. The timers are cleared on every scene change, which cancels any stale script for free. The platformer "World" handles walk, scooter, skateboard (auto-roll with kickflips, heelflips, tre flips, 360s, grabs and grinds) and bike modes. It also covers kicker ramps, rails, bouncy pool umbrellas, collectibles, triggers, Sunny's follow-and-hop AI, and an "interactables" system: stand next to the Toniebox and JUMP becomes PLAY ♪.

Designed so an 8-year-old can't lose

There are no fail states:

  • Dad's pitches get slower, then turn into a meatball any swing connects with.
  • Mom helps reel if the catfish fights too long.
  • An invisible safety net boings you back onto the trampoline.
  • The skate contest is "won" either way ("it's your BIRTHDAY, ssso you win!").

Beyond that:

  • Clue cards need an explicit "Got it!".
  • A Clue Book lets you flip back through clues.
  • Progress saves to localStorage, and ?reset wipes it for a fresh reveal.

How we worked (and tested)

  • Default and keep moving. Blaine sent about 59 messages while the AI was mid-build, often several a minute. Each one was folded in and restated in a line or two ("Mom judges the skate contest so both parents have an arc"). Nothing waited on a meeting. Assumptions were flagged and made one-line-configurable (js/config.js).
  • Stub the URL first. A spoiler-free page went up at the final address 40 minutes in, so the tinyurl, the custom domain and Pages were all proven before any game code mattered.
  • The AI looked at its own output. Headless Chrome (puppeteer-core) took screenshots of every level, mini-game and phone layout. Contact sheets of those went back to the model, which is how it caught a broken font, a roof texture bleeding into the sky, a hidden river, a bike that looked like dotted circles, and more.
  • A bot plays the whole story. An autoplay script spams A, teleports Isaac to each trigger, picks each path, and logs every scene change and console error. It ran after nearly every change, on both paths, and caught a real soft-lock that no human had hit yet.
  • Real phones, emulated. Pixel portrait/landscape with genuine touch events: tap the pad and assert that Isaac moved and jumped.
  • Three parallel reviewers. Subagents with different lenses played and read the build at the same time: a game critic (who wrote bots to measure timing windows), a kids-and-mobile UX expert, and a design/coherence and "quirky fun" critic (who audited story logic, pixel art and even the music data). Their findings drove the last few rounds: the reveal's OPEN button bug, the off-screen trampoline landings, worm-spam scoring, and about 35% of the dialog trimmed.
  • The best QA was Dad on his phone, plus a friend on iOS who found the stuck-zoom bug.

Bugs we caught (and how)

Pixel font showed 'FREID9'Three 3×5 glyphs (A, H, W) were 14 characters instead of 15; a padding hack hid it.Fixed the glyph strings and added a length check.
Tapping the speech bubble did nothingOnly taps on the game canvas advanced text; the dialog lives outside it in the DOM.Taps anywhere advance, plus a blinking NEXT ▶ pill that names the right button for the device.
Clue #1 flashed byThe same tap that finished Freida's battle dismissed the clue card.Clue cards need an explicit 'Got it!' button; a Clue Book shows every clue found.
Controller test got 'stuck'The test page's baseline was captured while the button used to wake the pad was held.Buttons always baseline as unpressed; only axes get a rest snapshot.
Top-right buttons dead on menusMenus are a full-screen overlay above the stage's stacking context.Moved the buttons out of the stage and above overlays.
iOS (Brave) got stuck zoomed iniOS ignores user-scalable=no.Block pinch/double-tap gestures and snap the viewport back if zoom slips through.
The reveal button said JUMP, not OPEN!The interaction system relabelled the A button every frame.Only relabel when the nearby interactable changes. Found by the UX reviewer.
Double-tap picked a choice he never sawChoice buttons appear under your finger and fired on the first pointerdown.Ignore choice taps for 0.4s after they appear.
Trampoline landings happened off-screenAt high POW the camera lagged about 0.15×vy behind.Faster camera while falling, plus look-ahead. Found by the game-critic bot.
Early presses caused BONKsFLIP is both 'time the landing' and 'start a flip'.A press that can't finish a flip counts as landing timing.

What we'd tell someone doing this

  • Personal details are the whole game. The Toniebox, the worm, a catfish bell, Brunsdale Fields, Mom's binoculars, the Pikachu hat: every specific detail Blaine added made it land harder than any mechanic.
  • Photos beat descriptions. House photos became pixel houses, and the fishing-hat photo became an unlockable hat.
  • Kid-proof the seams, not just the mechanics. The hardest bugs were at transitions: a tap that dismissed a clue, a label reset at the climax, a double-tap on a choice.
  • Guard the surprise. Nothing before the reveal says "trampoline": not the title, the URL, or the menus. The link to this page only appears after the mystery is solved.
  • Ship a URL in the first hour; ship a game in the second.
