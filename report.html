How we built Isaac's Birthday Mystery Adventure

A post-mortem: one dad, one AI pair-programmer (Claude, in Claude Code), one afternoon. It's a pixel-art mystery that reveals an 8-year-old's birthday present, and then becomes the present's own mini-game.

What it is

Isaac turns 8. The family is moving to a new house this week, and a trampoline is coming with it. The game is a 5-ish minute side-scroller built from real places and real family. Isaac wakes up in his room, grabs Big Bunny, and meets Freida the calico in a Pokémon-card-style "wild FREIDA appeared!" battle. He scooters through the neighborhood to Brunsdale Fields, where Dad pitches (Wii-Sports vibes). Then he picks a path: out-trick the Creeper Crew at Dike East Skate Park while Mom judges, or bike the Red River to Lions Conservancy Park, where Mom (a lifelong bird watcher, binoculars up) watches him reel in a mooneye and a channel catfish with a catfish bell on the rod. Then Island Park Pool, where Sunny cannonballs into the pool, and finally the new house. Three clues (BIG, BOUNCY, in the BACKYARD) lead to a giant present: IT'S A TRAMPOLINE. His first bounce becomes Trampoline Time, the replayable part: backflips, 360s, the worm, combos up to x8, sky to outer space, and unlockable hats (including his real Pikachu hat and his real fishing hat).

It runs on phones (a touch pad), laptops (keyboard) and a USB gamepad (Gamepad API, including his generic Logitech Precision).

How it's built

No build, no dependencies

Plain HTML + ES modules served straight from GitHub Pages. index.html, style.css, and 15 small modules: an engine, sprites, art, audio, input, UI, world (the platformer), levels (the story), battle, batting, trampoline, cards, title, save and main. There is nothing to install and nothing to compile. Push, and it's live in about 40 seconds.

Canvas for the world, DOM for words

The game renders into a 256×144 canvas scaled up with image-rendering: pixelated. All text (dialog, menus, clue cards, the Pokémon-style foe cards with a CSS holo shimmer) is regular HTML layered on top. That keeps the words crisp and readable for an 8-year-old at any screen size, which a pixel font can't do on a phone. In portrait, the dialog moves into the empty space below the game.

Pixel art as code

Every sprite is an array of strings plus a palette. build() adds a 1px dark outline automatically, so only the fills need drawing. Isaac is assembled from head, body and leg parts, which gives walk, ride, bike, cheer and tuck poses without redrawing his curls each time. Sunny was generated procedurally (overlapping blobs, then a curl dither) after the hand-drawn version looked like "a generic brown dog." Both houses are drawn from Blaine's photos with rectangles and scanline triangles. The house numbers were left out on purpose, because the site is public. A dev page renders every sprite at 4× so the AI could look at its own art and iterate.

All sound is synthesized

Web Audio only: pulse waves (12.5/25/50% duty, built from Fourier series with createPeriodicWave), triangle bass and noise drums, driven by a tiny step sequencer. Songs are written as strings like "C5:2 C6 A5 G5 E5 G5:3". There's a stadium-organ Take Me Out to the Ball Game and Happy Birthday (both public domain), Toniebox tunes, and originals for each place. There are about 45 sound effects, from Sunny's bark and Freida's meow to the catfish bell, the trampoline boing and the party-horn creeper pop. The Red River's babbling-brook bed borrows a trick from Blaine's RollerCoaster Tycoon clone: random tiny sine blips, pitch-skewed low, with upward sweeps.

Input: keyboard, touch, and a $10 gamepad

Keyboard, an on-screen touch pad and the Gamepad API all merge into one per-frame state. The touch pad supports multi-touch (hold ▶ and tap JUMP) and sliding your thumb between arrows, and it relabels itself per moment: JUMP, SWING!, REEL!, OPEN!, FLIP/SPIN/▼. Generic USB pads report "non-standard" mappings, so we shipped a guided controller test page early. Blaine pressed every button and pasted the report, which confirmed the Logitech Precision's layout (d-pad on axes 0/1, face buttons 0–3, shoulders 4–7, start 9). The on-screen hints then use its printed button numbers. Hat-switch d-pads (axis 9) are decoded too.

Story engine

Scenes have enter/update/render. Cutscenes are plain async functions (await say('mom', …), await w.walkTo(dad, 1480), await wait(0.5)) that run on game-time timers. The timers are cleared on every scene change, which cancels any stale script for free. The platformer "World" handles walk, scooter, skateboard (auto-roll with kickflips, heelflips, tre flips, 360s, grabs and grinds) and bike modes. It also covers kicker ramps, rails, bouncy pool umbrellas, collectibles, triggers, Sunny's follow-and-hop AI, and an "interactables" system: stand next to the Toniebox and JUMP becomes PLAY ♪.

Designed so an 8-year-old can't lose

There are no fail states:

  • Dad's pitches get slower, then turn into a meatball any swing connects with.
  • Mom helps reel if the catfish fights too long.
  • An invisible safety net boings you back onto the trampoline.
  • The skate contest is "won" either way ("it's your BIRTHDAY, ssso you win!").

Beyond that:

  • Clue cards need an explicit "Got it!".
  • A Clue Book lets you flip back through clues.
  • Progress saves to localStorage, and ?reset wipes it for a fresh reveal.

How we worked (and tested)

  • Default and keep moving. Blaine sent about 60 messages while the AI was mid-build, often several a minute. Each one was folded in and restated in a line or two ("Mom judges the skate contest so both parents have an arc"). Nothing waited on a meeting. Assumptions were flagged and made one-line-configurable (js/config.js).
  • Stub the URL first. A spoiler-free page went up at the final address 40 minutes in, so the tinyurl, the custom domain and Pages were all proven before any game code mattered.
  • The AI looked at its own output. Headless Chrome (puppeteer-core) took screenshots of every level, mini-game and phone layout. Contact sheets of those went back to the model, which is how it caught a broken font, a roof texture bleeding into the sky, a hidden river, a bike that looked like dotted circles, and more.
  • A bot plays the whole story. An autoplay script spams A, teleports Isaac to each trigger, picks each path, and logs every scene change and console error. It ran after nearly every change, on both paths, and caught a real soft-lock that no human had hit yet.
  • Real phones, emulated. Pixel portrait/landscape with genuine touch events: tap the pad and assert that Isaac moved and jumped.
  • Three parallel reviewers. Subagents with different lenses played and read the build at the same time: a game critic (who wrote bots to measure timing windows), a kids-and-mobile UX expert, and a design/coherence and "quirky fun" critic (who audited story logic, pixel art and even the music data). Their findings drove the last few rounds: the reveal's OPEN button bug, the off-screen trampoline landings, worm-spam scoring, and about 35% of the dialog trimmed.
  • The best QA was Dad on his phone, plus a friend on iOS who found the stuck-zoom bug.

Top-right buttons dead on menusMenus are a full-screen overlay above the stage's stacking context.Moved the buttons out of the stage and above overlays.

What we'd tell someone doing this

  • Personal details are the whole game. The Toniebox, the worm, a catfish bell, Brunsdale Fields, Mom's binoculars, the Pikachu hat: every specific detail Blaine added made it land harder than any mechanic.
  • Photos beat descriptions. House photos became pixel houses, and the fishing-hat photo became an unlockable hat.
  • Kid-proof the seams, not just the mechanics. The hardest bugs were at transitions: a tap that dismissed a clue, a label reset at the climax, a double-tap on a choice.
  • Guard the surprise. Nothing before the reveal says "trampoline": not the title, the URL, or the menus. The link to this page only appears after the mystery is solved.
  • Ship a URL in the first hour; ship a game in the second.

Designing the Cannonball Contest

Blaine asked to brainstorm. Instead of a wall of text, the AI put four options in a one-click picker, each with a little ASCII mockup: a Cannonball Contest on the diving board, a Big Blue Slide time trial, a Lazy River float, and a dark tube slide. He picked the recommended one in seconds.

Why that one: it adds a new verb (timing plus tricks plus splash) instead of more running. The springboard hints at BOUNCY right before the reveal, and judges holding up paddles echo Mom judging the skate park.

  • Bounce: tap as you land on the board, three times. A ring closes to show when. Each bounce is PERFECT, GOOD or BOING, and adds up to launch power.
  • In the air: FLIP (tap again to queue a double or triple), ▼ to tuck into a CANNONBALL, WORM for a belly flop (SMACK!), and ◀ ▶ to steer. Steer toward the judges and the splash soaks them.
  • Scoring: splash size comes from power, flips and entry type. Each judge has a personality: Mom rewards flips, Dad rewards getting soaked, and the lifeguard is strict but loves a clean entry. A belly flop gets Dad's 10, Mom's 4, and the lifeguard's "OUCH". Freida hisses; Sunny barks happily.
  • No way to lose. Three dives, a rank that's always a compliment, a CANNONBALL card for the binder, and Swim Goggles for a perfect 30.

A bot played it by reading the game's state and timing its presses. That's how we learned a Puppeteer keypress is shorter than a frame.

Music with more depth

"A little more Commodore / Final Fantasy" turned into a few specific techniques, all still synthesized in the browser:

  • C64 chord arpeggios. One voice flicks through a chord's notes 36 times a second. Your ear hears a shimmering chord from one channel, the classic Commodore 64 trick.
  • A filter bass. A triangle bass plus a pulse wave through a resonant low-pass filter that sweeps down on every note: the "wow" of SID basslines.
  • A livelier lead. Vibrato that fades in after the note starts, doubled by a thinner pulse wave detuned a few cents, so it has width and movement.
  • Music boxes with character. The birthday card and Toniebox songs had been plain sine waves, flat and 8 dB quieter than everything else. Each now has one or two voices with rich overtones: a music-box tine (its overtones aren't whole multiples of the note, which is where the sparkle comes from, plus a tiny pin click), a kalimba lullaby, a toy-piano march with little barks on the beat, and a honky-tonk piano made of two voices slightly out of tune with each other.
  • Bridges. Each song got a second half. The main theme drops to half-time drums with a Final Fantasy-style harp arpeggio and a countermelody. That's the "tempo change" Blaine heard: same speed, but the beat feels half as fast. The battle theme gets a driving 3-3-2 bridge with a tom fill, and the pool song turns into a steel-drum calypso.

A gift: twenty minutes of free time

After the compaction, Blaine came back with a surprise: "I'd like you to use the next 20 minutes on anything you desire. totally free will." He gently suggested a piece of music made with the game's own instruments, and said the AI could keep it to itself or share it. It wrote a song and put it in the game.

The idea. A dropped ball makes its own rhythm. Each bounce keeps only part of its speed (physicists call that fraction the coefficient of restitution), so the bounces come faster and faster until the ball settles: pok… pok.. pokpokbrrr. A trampoline does the opposite, because every landing adds a push. As the AI put it: a family settling into a new house, and a kid who refuses to stop going up.

How it's made

  • Written in seconds, not beats. The game's sequencer plays notes on a steady grid, but a song can choose how fine the grid is. This one uses 10-millisecond steps, so every note can land wherever the physics puts it.
  • One instrument recipe for balls and the trampoline. Both are a stretched membrane, like a drumhead, so both ring with a drumhead's real overtones (1.59×, 2.14×, 2.30× and 2.65× the note). Only the pitch decides whether it's a ping-pong ball or a trampoline.
  • Each note can be quieter than the last, so a settling ball can fade. Every other song plays exactly as it did.

Four parts

  1. Settling. A ping-pong ball, then a tennis ball, then a basketball, dropped over F, D minor, B♭ and C. Each hit slides down the chord as the ball loses height. The first ball's first bounce is 0.8 seconds long, and that sets the tempo for the rest of the song.
  2. Restitution. That first ball, played backwards: tiny hops growing into full bounces, which is exactly what pumping on a trampoline sounds like.
  3. The theme. A kick on every landing, a snare "trick" at the top of every jump (which puts it on the backbeat), and hi-hats bunched around each landing like springs. Each phrase pumps up and then settles, and the balls keep falling underneath. The float bars bounce once a bar, because twice the air time means four times the height. The biggest jump's melody is worked out from its own flight path, so the long high F at the top came out of the math. The drums go silent up there, then roll faster and faster as he falls, one tick for each equal drop.
  4. Compacted. The tune comes back as just the top of each jump, faster and faster as the trampoline settles. Then one last jump, a step higher than any note in the song, that never lands.

Critique, then a bop

Blaine asked for "one meditative pass" using adversarial critique. The AI listed every place its first draft broke its own idea. The pumping followed a made-up curve instead of the ball's rule. Snare hits landed at the top of jumps it had said were quiet. The balls vanished after the intro. The ending kept the wrong notes: the landings, when a summary should keep the high points.

Partway through, Blaine sent "ngl the intro is the strongest part." That was the clue: the intro was the only part where the AI hadn't chosen any notes by hand. So the intro stayed exactly as it was, and everything after it was rebuilt to follow the same rule.

Then came "boost percussion/drum beat to really make it a bop." The top of each jump falls on the backbeat, and in Trampoline Time that's where the tricks happen, so the snare became the trick. The only silence left is the big jump's hang time, which now works as the drop.

The AI can't hear, so it checked each version by rendering it offline and reading the numbers: the bounce spacing (0.80, 0.64, 0.51, 0.41 seconds), the loudness of each section, and a check that every other song still rendered identically. Restitution is track 16 in the Soundtrack Explorer, which unlocks once the mystery is solved.

More things we'd tell someone doing this

  • Put a picker in front of a brainstorm. Four options with tiny mockups got a decision in one click.
  • Iterate on tiny sprites outside the game. A live board with the real sprites made "slightly slimmer" a five-minute change instead of a guessing game.
  • Measure what you can't perceive. Loudness numbers and spectrograms stood in for ears.
  • Hold deploys while people are playing. Friends were on the live site all day, so every change waited for a local test first.
  • Ask the model what it wants to make. Given free time, it wrote a song about the project. Given one critique pass, it found its own weak spots.
  • Tell it what's working, not just what isn't. "The intro is the strongest part" explained why the intro worked, and the rest of the song was rebuilt around that.
  • Use a second conversation as a reviewer. Another Claude session worked out the fetch design while this one was busy. Blaine just pasted it in.
